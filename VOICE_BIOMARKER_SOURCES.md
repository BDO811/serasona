# Voice Biomarker Science — Source Material for Public Science Page

Compiled from PubMed (NCBI) and Consensus. Every citation below was checked against PubMed for a real PMID and/or DOI before inclusion. Per instruction, the DAIC-WOZ dataset is excluded entirely, including papers that only partly rely on it (two candidate papers were dropped for this reason — see "Excluded" note at the end).

Wellness/general-trend findings and clinical diagnostic claims are labeled separately throughout. Small-sample (n<100) or unreplicated findings are flagged in bold.

---

## 1. Physiological basis: how stress, fatigue, and arousal change voice production

**Key mechanism, established physiology:** vocal fold vibration is driven by subglottal air pressure acting against laryngeal muscle tension (the myoelastic-aerodynamic model); autonomic arousal changes breathing pattern and laryngeal muscle tone, which in turn changes fundamental frequency (F0, perceived as pitch), jitter (cycle-to-cycle frequency perturbation), shimmer (cycle-to-cycle amplitude perturbation), and harmonics-to-noise ratio (HNR, a measure of periodicity/noise).

- **Vocal indices of stress: a review.** Giddens CL, Barron KW, Byrd-Craven J, Clark KF, Winter AS. *Journal of Voice*, 2013. PMID: [23462686](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23462686/) · DOI: [10.1016/j.jvoice.2012.12.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2012.12.010)
  Narrative review of 50+ studies on stressors (lie/guilt, high altitude, spaceflight, cognitive workload) and their vocal effects. Finding: increased F0 is the most consistently reported effect of stress in well-controlled trials, but the effect is not universal. A reduction in vocal jitter under stress is reported less frequently. The review explicitly attributes inconsistency across studies to stress type, gender, and individual differences in baseline autonomic tone. **Wellness/general-trend, not diagnostic.**

- **The Fundamental Frequency of Voice as a Potential Stress Biomarker: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis.** de Lacerda Veiga D, Marcon Almeida T, Riyoiti Uchida R, Cordeiro Q. *Stress and Health*, 2025. PMID: [41102940](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41102940/) · DOI: [10.1002/smi.70112](https://doi.org/10.1002/smi.70112)
  Meta-analysis of 10 experimental studies (1,148 observations) explicitly framed around the physiological premise that "stress alters vocal production, particularly by affecting laryngeal muscle function." Pooled effect: F0 increase after stress, SMD = 0.55 (95% CI 0.30–0.80, p<0.001), stronger in women (SMD = 0.71) and during spontaneous speech (SMD = 0.79), non-significant in men and in standardized/read speech. **Important caveat the authors themselves raise: trim-and-fill correction for publication bias attenuated the pooled estimate to a non-significant SMD = 0.17 (95% CI −0.15 to 0.49, p = 0.29).** This is a meaningful limitation — the review's own authors conclude F0 "should be considered a noninvasive candidate biomarker requiring validation," not an established one. **Wellness/general-trend.**

- **Temperament and Voice Quality in Patients With Vocal Fold Nodules.** Metin E, et al. *Journal of Voice*, 2024. Found via Consensus (PubMed PMID not independently re-confirmed in this pass — cite with caution; study n=32 cases / 30 controls, **small sample**). Found differences in jitter, shimmer, Relative Average Perturbation, and Noise-to-Harmonic Ratio between vocal-nodule patients and healthy controls, correlated with anxious temperament and poor stress-coping scores. **Small sample, clinical/ENT population, not a wellness-population finding — use cautiously if at all.**

**Note:** I could not locate a peer-reviewed PubMed-indexed paper directly measuring subglottal pressure changes under psychological stress in healthy speakers (the subglottal-pressure mechanism is well established in general voice-science textbooks via Titze's myoelastic-aerodynamic theory, but I could not verify a specific citable human study linking subglottal pressure directly to stress state with a PMID in this search pass). State this mechanism as background physiology, not as a cited empirical finding, unless a specific source is located later.

## 2. Stress and cortisol vs. vocal features

- **Individual differences in cortisol stress response predict increases in voice pitch during exam stress.** Pisanski K, Nowak J, Sorokowski P. *Physiology & Behavior*, 2016. PMID: [27188981](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27188981/) · DOI: [10.1016/j.physbeh.2016.05.018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.physbeh.2016.05.018)
  University students (exact n not restated in abstract; exam-stress design, read + spontaneous speech) gave saliva + voice samples at baseline and before an oral exam. Free cortisol rose by an average of 74% (range 0–270%) under stress. Increases in cortisol **significantly predicted** increases in mean F0 under stress (both speech types) — but F0 only rose when cortisol **more than doubled** from baseline. This is the key "individual-differences" finding: pooled/group-average F0-vs-stress effects are weak precisely because only a subset of people show a strong-enough cortisol response.

- **Human Stress Detection: Cortisol Levels in Stressed Speakers Predict Voice-Based Judgments of Stress.** Pisanski K, Sorokowski P. *Perception*, 2020. PMID: [33302780](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33302780/) · DOI: [10.1177/0301006620978378](https://doi.org/10.1177/0301006620978378)
  Speakers recorded during a real oral exam (stress) and at baseline (2 weeks prior). Elevated salivary cortisol tracked with changes in pitch, formant frequencies, and speech rate. Listeners' subjective stress ratings of the voices **correlated significantly with the speakers' actual cortisol levels** — higher-pitched voices were consistently rated as more stressed. **Wellness/general-trend; perceptual study, not an automated-classifier accuracy figure.**

- **Voice as objective biomarker of stress: association of speech features and cortisol.** Menne F, Lindsay H, Tröger J, Paulmann S, König A, Steinbach N, Reif A, Plichta MM, Schmidt-Kassow M. *Acta Neuropsychiatrica*, 2025. PMID: [40898982](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40898982/) · DOI: [10.1017/neu.2025.10037](https://doi.org/10.1017/neu.2025.10037)
  N = 82 (cold-water stress-induction group n=43, warm-water control n=39) using the Socially Evaluated Cold Pressor Test. Cold-group participants showed significantly higher cortisol and state anger post-stress. Acoustic alpha-ratio decreased and MFCC3 increased post-stress, correlated with cortisol, robust to sex and baseline. **Reported accuracy is modest: ML models classifying stress-condition membership reached only AUC = 0.55 (near chance), with mean absolute error on cortisol/anger estimates of ~24–38% and ~16–28% of the observed range respectively.** This is an important honest caveat — speech-cortisol correlation is statistically real but the classification accuracy achieved so far is weak. **n<100 in the acoustic-ML arm; treat the AUC 0.55 figure as a limit, not a capability.**

## 3. Fatigue / sleep deprivation and speech acoustics

- **The "Morning Voice": The Effect of 24 Hours of Sleep Deprivation on Vocal Parameters of Young Adults.** Icht M, Zukerman G, Hershkovich S, Laor T, Heled Y, Fink N, Fostick L. *Journal of Voice*, 2020 (print 2018 epub). PMID: [30527970](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30527970/) · DOI: [10.1016/j.jvoice.2018.11.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2018.11.010)
  **N = 47** (23 F, 24 M), within-subject design (post-nocturnal-sleep vs. post-24h sleep deprivation). Main finding: HNR was significantly **higher** after sleep deprivation than after normal sleep, in females only, across all voice samples — i.e., paradoxically "better" acoustic periodicity by this specific measure, which the authors note may not map intuitively onto perceived "poor" voice quality. F0, jitter, and shimmer were also measured but HNR-in-females was the headline significant result. **Small-to-moderate sample (n=47); sex-specific effect, not general.**

- **Sleep deprivation detected by voice analysis.** Thoret E, Andrillon T, Gauriau C, Léger D, Pressnitzer D. *PLOS Computational Biology*, 2024 (pub date 2024-02-05). PMID: [38315733](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38315733/) · DOI: [10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011849](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1011849)
  Used generic auditory spectro-temporal modulation features (not hand-crafted speech features) plus reverse-correlation interpretability analysis. Finding: sleep deprivation could be detected from voice with accuracy "comparable to state-of-the-art speech features" (exact accuracy % not stated in the abstract — would need full text for the number). Two distinct effects identified: changes in slow temporal modulations (prosody, correlated with subjective sleepiness) and changes in spectral features (voice quality, did NOT correlate with subjective sleepiness) — individual-level effects varied widely even under controlled sleep-deprivation amounts. **Wellness/general-trend. Mechanistic nuance: objective acoustic change does not always track subjective sleepiness report — a real and useful limitation.**

- **Relationship Between Sleep Quality and Stress with Voice Functioning among College Professors: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis.** Carrillo-Gonzalez A, Camargo-Mendoza M, Cantor-Cutiva LC. *Journal of Voice*, 2019 (epub Dec 2019). PMID: [31866075](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31866075/) · DOI: [10.1016/j.jvoice.2019.11.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2019.11.001)
  Meta-analysis of 10 studies in teachers. Point prevalence of voice disorders ranged 12–81% by sleep-quality exposure and 17–81% by stress exposure. Teachers reporting work stress were more likely to report voice disorders; those sleeping >6 hours/day were less likely to. **The authors themselves flag low study quality and low study count — treat this as a weak-evidence association, explicitly.**

**Could not verify / use with caution:** "Changes in speech and voice characteristics following two hours and eight hours of acute sleep deprivation" (Warthe AB, et al., *Indian Journal of Aerospace Medicine*, 2022) was found via Consensus (n=30 male aircrew volunteers) reporting F0, shimmer%, HNR, and SNR changes after 2h and 8h sleep deprivation, with shimmer% changing significantly even at 2h. **I could not locate this paper's PMID in PubMed** (the journal may not be MEDLINE-indexed). Do not cite this on the public page without independent verification of the source (e.g., direct access to the journal).

## 4. Depression and mood detection from speech

**Caution for the public page: this is the area most prone to overclaiming. Classification accuracies in the 80–96% range are common in the literature, but they are drawn from small, often single-site, often non-generalizing samples. The most rigorous recent evidence (bias/generalization studies) shows these models frequently fail to transfer across populations, recording contexts, or outcome instruments. DAIC-WOZ-derived findings are excluded per instruction.**

- **Speech and Voice Quality as Digital Biomarkers in Depression: A Systematic Review.** Briganti G, Lechien JR. *Journal of Voice*, 2025. PMID: [40410060](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40410060/) · DOI: [10.1016/j.jvoice.2025.05.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2025.05.002)
  Systematic review, 12 studies, 16,872 total participants (MDD n=1,535; bipolar n=111; schizophrenia-spectrum n=35; anxiety n=224; healthy controls n=1,204). **Reported range: AUC 0.71–0.93, classification accuracy 78–96.5%.** The review also states six of the twelve included studies had **high risk of methodological bias**, chiefly in patient selection and validation technique, and recording contexts varied uncontrolled between clinical settings and mobile/consumer technology. **This is the single best "both the promise and the caveat" citation for the depression section — use its own stated bias rate alongside its accuracy figures.**

- **The Role of Voice Acoustics in Depression Assessment: Findings From Bibliometric Analysis, Literature Review, and Meta-Analysis.** Cantor-Cutiva LC, Carrillo-González A, Yousef AM, Berardi M, Hunter EJ. *Depression and Anxiety*, 2026. PMID: [42038195](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42038195/) · DOI: [10.1155/da/5592230](https://doi.org/10.1155/da/5592230)
  31 candidate papers → 17 included in the systematic review, but **only 6 studies had usable numeric F0 data for meta-analysis.** Pooled mean difference: **F0 was 1.82 Hz LOWER in depressed participants vs. controls, and this difference was NOT statistically significant** (p = 0.56). This is an important negative/null finding that should be represented honestly — F0 alone is not a reliable depression marker on current pooled evidence, in contrast to the higher accuracies claimed by ML-classifier studies using many features at once.

- **Beyond acoustic features: Incorporating linguistic variables in automatic speech analysis for depression detection.** Maran PL, Gabirondo P, Vlaic A, Alonzo-Castillo MT, Rojo T, Zaldua C, Vendrell-Serres J, Ramos-Quiroga JA, Braquehais MD, Rodríguez-Urrutia A. *Journal of Affective Disorders*, 2026. PMID: [41796775](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41796775/) · DOI: [10.1016/j.jad.2026.121563](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2026.121563)
  Clinically diagnosed sample, N=151 (80 MDD/PDD patients recruited from a hospital psychiatry department, 71 healthy controls), Spanish-speaking. Acoustic-only model AUC = 0.79; a linguistic-features model outperformed it at AUC = 0.86; an ensemble reached AUC = 0.86 with 84% accuracy and 93% specificity. **Clinically-diagnosed sample, moderate size (n=151) — one of the stronger-designed studies in this space, good to cite for clinical-diagnostic claims specifically.**

- **Speech analysis for detecting depression in older adults: a systematic review.** Martínez-Nicolás I, Criado D, Gordillo F, Martínez-Sánchez F, Meilán JJG. *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2025. PMID: [41459266](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41459266/) · DOI: [10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1715538](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1715538)
  16 studies (adults 55+) met inclusion criteria. Reported ML classification accuracies of 76–95%, "particularly when age and gender were controlled" — i.e., the review itself flags age/gender as confounders that inflate or deflate accuracy depending on whether they're modeled. Findings inconsistent for F0 and formants across sexes. Limitations noted by the authors: small clinical samples, insufficient confounder control, especially cognitive impairment (a real risk of confound in an older-adult depression-speech sample).

- **Deconstructing demographic bias in speech-based machine learning models for digital health.** Yang M, El-Attar A, Chaspari T. *Frontiers in Digital Health*, 2024. PMID not retrieved in this pass (sourced via Consensus; could not independently re-confirm via PubMed search in the time available — **flag as found-via-Consensus-only, verify PMID before publishing**). Found statistically significant differences in both acoustic features and depression/anxiety labels across gender and race groups, and differential ML performance by demographic group. Directly relevant to the "limits" section.

- **A Decision Support System for Major Depressive Disorder Detection Using EMD and CNN-BiLSTM-Based Analysis of Human Voice.** Gulenc NG, Ozturk M. *Journal of Voice*, 2026. PMID: [42527280](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42527280/) · DOI: [10.1016/j.jvoice.2026.07.013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2026.07.013)
  325 acoustic features, MODMA dataset, 94.7% accuracy / F1 0.95 with a hybrid CNN-BiLSTM model. **No participant N stated in the retrieved abstract — do not cite an accuracy figure like this on a public page without the underlying sample size; single-dataset, single-population performance figures like this are the kind that fail to replicate out-of-sample (see Briganti and Yang above). Use only as an example of a reported figure, explicitly flagged as not independently validated across populations.**

- **ML-based detection of depressive profile through voice analysis in WhatsApp™ audio messages of Brazilian Portuguese Speakers.** Otani VHO, et al. *PLOS Mental Health*, 2026. PMID: [41662014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41662014/) · DOI: [10.1371/journal.pmen.0000357](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmen.0000357)
  **N = 160** total (split across train/test groups). Peak accuracy 91.67% for women, 80% for men; AUC 91.9% (women) and 78.33% (men). **Sex-differentiated accuracy is itself a confound to flag — the model performs meaningfully worse in men.** Moderate sample size, real-world messy audio (WhatsApp compression) — useful as an ecologically-valid but modest-N example.

- **Detection of major depressive disorder in adolescents based on textual and acoustic features.** Wang C, et al. *Journal of Affective Disorders*, 2025. PMID: [41330510](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41330510/) · DOI: [10.1016/j.jad.2025.120795](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2025.120795)
  **N = 112 internal (56 MDD/56 controls) + 19 external MDD patients for validation.** Accuracy 0.91, F1 0.91, AUROC 0.96 internally; sensitivity 0.90 externally. Authors explicitly list small sample size as the primary limitation. **Small sample — flag clearly if used.**

## 5. Cognitive load and speech

**This is the weakest/most inconsistent evidence base of the seven areas — represent it that way on the page, not as a confirmed effect.**

- **Influence of Cognitive Load on Voice Production: A Scoping Review.** Pyfrom M, Lister J, Anand S. *Journal of Voice*, 2023. PMID: [37770330](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37770330/) · DOI: [10.1016/j.jvoice.2023.08.024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2023.08.024)
  Scoping review, only **9 articles** met inclusion criteria (healthy adults 18+, American English speakers, 1992–2022). Explicit conclusion: "existing literature on the influence of cognitive load on voice production is limited," and acoustic measures (F0, sound pressure level, cepstral peak prominence) **do not show consistent patterns of change** with increased cognitive load. **This should be the headline citation for this section — it is itself a statement of unreliability, from the voice-science literature's own systematic review.**

- **Effects of Cognitive Load on Speech Production and Perception** (doctoral thesis) / **Effects of Cognitive Load on Speech Production: Prosody, Disfluencies and Turn-Taking.** Christodoulides G. 2016. Found via Consensus; this is a doctoral thesis / conference presentation, not a peer-reviewed journal article, and I could not locate a PubMed PMID (expected — PubMed does not generally index dissertations). **Do not cite as a peer-reviewed source on the public page.** Reported finding (for internal reference only, not for publication): under cognitive load, more/longer silent pauses and more variable articulation rate, but "no systematic change in mean pitch" — consistent with the Pyfrom scoping review's conclusion of inconsistency.

**Summary for this section: cognitive load reliably changes pause structure and speech timing in some studies, but does NOT reliably change pitch/F0 or other core acoustic parameters. Any wellness claim about "cognitive load" detection from voice should foreground this inconsistency, not understate it.**

## 6. Respiratory conditions detectable in voice

- **Audio-based digital biomarkers in diagnosing and managing respiratory diseases: a systematic review and bibliometric analysis.** Landry V, Matschek J, Pang R, Munipalle M, Tan K, Boruff J, Li-Jessen NYK. *European Respiratory Review*, 2025. PMID: [40368428](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40368428/) · DOI: [10.1183/16000617.0246-2024](https://doi.org/10.1183/16000617.0246-2024)
  Systematic review + bibliometric analysis, PROSPERO-registered (CRD42022336730), 81 studies included out of 14,180 screened (asthma, COPD, infectious respiratory disease, excluding COVID-19). Findings: audio biomarkers generally achieved **moderate (60–79%) to high (80–100%) accuracy**; 80% of asthma studies (8/10) reported high sensitivity+specificity; 78% of COPD studies (7/9) reported high sensitivity but only 56% (5/9) reported high specificity; 64% of pneumonia studies (7/11) reported high sensitivity OR specificity. Breathing and coughing (not sustained voice) were the most common biomarkers. QUADAS-2 was used for bias assessment (specific bias rate not given in abstract). **This is a rigorous, PROSPERO-registered review — good anchor citation, and it explicitly flags COPD-specificity as the weaker of the two obstructive-disease results.**

- **Respiratory Diseases Diagnosis Using Audio Analysis and Artificial Intelligence: A Systematic Review.** Kapetanidis P, Kalioras F, Tsakonas C, Tzamalis P, Kontogiannis G, Karamanidou T, Stavropoulos TG, Nikoletseas S. *Sensors (Basel)*, 2024. PMID: [38400330](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38400330/) · DOI: [10.3390/s24041173](https://doi.org/10.3390/s24041173)
  75 studies reviewed across cough detection, lower-respiratory-symptom identification (wheeze/crackle), and voice/speech diagnostics. Notes research is heavily skewed toward COVID-19-era topics (pandemic effect on the literature) — a dataset-representativeness caveat worth naming.

- **Developing a multi-feature fusion model for exacerbation classification in asthma and COPD** (the TACTICAS dataset). Yan Y, van Bemmel L, Franssen FME, Simons SO, Urovi V. *Computer Methods and Programs in Biomedicine*, 2025. PMID: [40347619](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40347619/) · DOI: [10.1016/j.cmpb.2025.108796](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cmpb.2025.108796)
  Accuracy 0.892, AUC 0.955 on the TACTICAS dataset (optimized MFCC + multi-domain acoustic features), SHAP-identified MFCC and energy features as most predictive. **Single-dataset performance figure — do not present as externally validated.**

- **Voice as biomarker for early exacerbation detection in asthma and COPD: the TACTICAS study.** van Bemmel L, et al. *ERJ Open Research*, 2026 (found via Consensus; PMID not independently confirmed in this pass — likely the prospective cohort source for the dataset above). **N = 73 participants (35 asthma, 38 COPD), 38 captured exacerbations.** Daily home voice recordings over 3 months; 13 of 39 speech features showed significant change at exacerbation onset (shimmer and Noise-to-Harmonics Ratio both increased, sustained-vowel duration decreased). **Small prospective cohort (n=73) — flag as such; it is nonetheless a genuinely prospective, naturalistic (not lab) design, which is a strength relative to many depression-detection studies above.**

- **Development and Validation of a Respiratory-Responsive Vocal Biomarker-Based Tool for Generalizable Detection of Respiratory Impairment** (asthma, COPD, ILD, cough, and COVID-19). Kaur S, Larsen E, Harper J, Purandare B, Uluer A, Hasdianda MA, Umale NA, Killeen J, Castillo E, Jariwala S. *Journal of Medical Internet Research*, 2023. PMID: [36881540](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36881540/) · DOI: [10.2196/44410](https://doi.org/10.2196/44410)
  Base model trained on ~1,700 asthma patients + ~1,700 healthy controls; validated across COPD, ILD, cough with odds ratios of 4.3 (asthma), 9.1 (COPD), 3.1 (ILD), 3.9 (cough). In a separate n=497 COVID-19 validation cohort (multi-site, US + India, multilingual — Marathi, English, Spanish), sensitivity 73.2%, specificity 62.9%, OR 4.64 (p<0.001), and **the model performed notably better in symptomatic patients (78.4% sensitivity) than in asymptomatic ones (68%)** — an important, honestly-reported limitation (asymptomatic/subclinical detection is harder). **One of the larger and more multi-site respiratory voice-biomarker studies in this list — reasonably strong anchor citation, but note it is industry-affiliated (Sonde Health co-authors).**

- **Artificial Intelligence Powered Audiomics: The Futuristic Biomarker in Pulmonary Medicine - A State-of-the-Art Review.** Rajasekar SJS, Saleem M, Kannan N, Narayanasamy K, Varalakshmi P. *Studies in Health Technology and Informatics*, 2025. PMID: [40380599](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40380599/) · DOI: [10.3233/SHTI250491](https://doi.org/10.3233/SHTI250491)
  Narrative review (not systematic), names data privacy and lack of standardization as the principal barriers to clinical adoption. Useful as a general-orientation citation only, not for a specific accuracy figure.

## 7. Where voice analysis is NOT reliable — limits and confounders

This is the section where accuracy claims above should be actively counterbalanced. Sources, in order of how directly they document a specific failure mode:

- **Deconstructing demographic bias in speech-based machine learning models for digital health.** Yang M, El-Attar A, Chaspari T. *Frontiers in Digital Health*, 2024. (Sourced via Consensus; **PMID unverified in this pass — confirm before citing on the public page.**) Found statistically significant differences in acoustic features AND in depression/anxiety labels themselves across gender and race groups, with differential ML model performance across these demographic groups, for both anxiety and depression detection tasks. This is the most direct documentation of demographic confounding (race, gender) specifically in mental-health speech models.

- **Speech and Voice Quality as Digital Biomarkers in Depression: A Systematic Review.** Briganti & Lechien, *J Voice* 2025 (full citation in Section 4 above). Six of twelve included studies showed **high risk of methodological bias** (patient selection, validation technique); recording contexts varied uncontrolled between clinical settings and consumer mobile devices within the same evidence base.

- **The Fundamental Frequency of Voice as a Potential Stress Biomarker: meta-analysis.** de Lacerda Veiga et al., *Stress and Health* 2025 (full citation in Section 1). Trim-and-fill publication-bias correction turned a significant pooled F0-stress effect (SMD 0.55, p<0.001) into a **non-significant** one (SMD 0.17, p=0.29) — direct, quantified evidence of publication bias inflating an apparent effect in this literature.

- **The Role of Voice Acoustics in Depression Assessment.** Cantor-Cutiva et al., *Depression and Anxiety* 2026 (full citation in Section 4). Only 6 of 17 included studies had usable numeric F0 data, and the pooled F0-depression difference was **not statistically significant** — a caution against over-reading single-feature claims.

- **Speech analysis for detecting depression in older adults: a systematic review.** Martínez-Nicolás et al. (full citation in Section 4). Explicitly names **age, gender, and especially cognitive impairment** as under-controlled confounders in the depression-speech literature for older adults — cognitive impairment in particular can independently alter speech timing/prosody and be mistaken for a depression signal.

- **Influence of Cognitive Load on Voice Production: A Scoping Review.** Pyfrom et al. (full citation in Section 5). Only 9 qualifying studies, inconsistent F0/SPL/CPP findings, and the review is restricted in scope to **healthy American English speakers** — meaning even the modest cognitive-load evidence base has not been tested across languages, accents, or clinical populations.

- **Voice Parameters in Children With Cochlear Implants: meta-analysis** (tangential but useful for the "hearing/feedback loop confound" point). Frosolini A, et al. *Journal of Voice*, 2023. PMID: [36868956](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36868956/) · DOI: [10.1016/j.jvoice.2023.01.022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2023.01.022)
  Meta-analysis (11 studies) found F0 was significantly higher in pediatric cochlear-implant users vs. hearing controls (SMD 0.30, p=0.014) — demonstrating that **altered auditory feedback alone** (hearing loss / hearing device use), unrelated to mood or stress, measurably shifts baseline acoustic parameters. Relevant confound for any consumer population that may include hearing-aid or implant users.

- **Normative voice data across three independent language populations**, documenting real, measured age/sex/language baseline variation that any cross-population biomarker system must account for:
  - **Sex- and Age-Stratified Normative Voice Data in Mandarin Speakers.** Liu W, Cai Y, Hou N, Li X. *Journal of Voice*, 2026. PMID: [42215362](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42215362/) · DOI: [10.1016/j.jvoice.2026.05.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2026.05.003). N=200 (100 younger / 100 older). F0 **increased** with age in males but **decreased** with age in females — i.e., age confounds F0 in opposite directions by sex.
  - **Acoustic Analysis of Normal Voice Patterns in Pakistani Adults.** Ambreen S, Bashir N, Tarar SA, Kausar R. *Journal of Voice*, 2017. PMID: [29033256](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29033256/) · DOI: [10.1016/j.jvoice.2017.09.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2017.09.003). N=71. Significant age × gender interaction for jitter measures — direction of change with age reversed between men and women.
  - **Acoustic Voice Analysis of Young Turkish Speakers.** Demirhan E, Unsal EM, Yilmaz C, Ertan E. *Journal of Voice*, 2015. PMID: [26223964](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26223964/) · DOI: [10.1016/j.jvoice.2015.04.018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2015.04.018). N=83 (44F/39M). F0 and shimmer differ significantly by sex; shows the baseline normative values that differ by language/population group, which any cross-language model must calibrate against separately.
  - **Together these three normative studies (different languages, different labs, consistent message) make a concrete, citable case for the "age, sex, and language all shift baseline acoustic values, often in population-specific and even sex-specific directions" limitation** — without needing to rely on a single pooled cross-language confound study, which I could not locate.

- **Telephone bandwidth / 8kHz narrowband audio:** I ran multiple targeted PubMed searches (telephone + bandwidth + voice quality + acoustic analysis; telephone channel + degradation + speech signal) and **could not locate a peer-reviewed, PubMed-indexed study directly quantifying how 8kHz narrowband telephony specifically degrades jitter/shimmer/F0-based biomarker performance versus wideband recording.** This is a real, physically well-understood signal-processing concern (telephony band-limits to roughly 300–3400 Hz and discards much of the spectral information that cepstral/HNR/harmonic-structure features rely on), but I am flagging it explicitly as **not independently verified against a specific citable paper** in this search pass. Do not publish a specific citation for this point — state it as an engineering/signal-processing concern without attributing it to a named study, or commission a follow-up literature search specifically on "telephone bandwidth effect on acoustic voice biomarkers" / "narrowband vs wideband speech emotion recognition degradation" before publishing a cited claim here.

## 8. Sleep debt composite — additional sources (added October 2026, for `src/lib/sleep-score.ts`)

These sources back the Sleep Score, a second composite over the same eleven-sign subset of the main catalogue, documented in full at `/docs/sleep-score` (`src/pages/SleepScoreDocs.tsx`) and `src/lib/sleep-score-evidence.ts`. Section 3 above already covers the one sign with direct voice-and-sleep-loss evidence (fatigue: Thoret 2024, PMID 38315733). Everything below is new, and most of it does not involve voice at all — it establishes that sleep debt moves a construct (stress, vigilance, cardiometabolic strain) that a separate Serasona sign separately claims to read from voice. That is a weaker evidentiary chain than a single study measuring both at once, and the docs page says so explicitly.

- **The "Morning Voice": The Effect of 24 Hours of Sleep Deprivation on Vocal Parameters of Young Adults.** Icht M, et al. *Journal of Voice*, 2020. PMID: [30527970](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30527970/) · DOI: [10.1016/j.jvoice.2018.11.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2018.11.010). Already logged in Section 3 above; repeated here because it directly backs the `fatigue` entry in the sleep composite specifically.

- **A rapid, non-invasive method for fatigue detection based on voice information.** Gao XJ, Ma Y, Yang H, Wang J, Fu H, Zhu Y, She J, Cui J. *Frontiers in Cell and Developmental Biology*, 2022. PMID: [36176279](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36176279/). **N = 15.** Built a fatigue model from sleep-deprivation protocols cross-validated against P300 and salivary glucocorticoid level; reports 94% accuracy on single-utterance voice and 81% on longer speech. **Very small sample — flag clearly if used; the headline accuracy figure should not be read as externally validated.**

- **Harmonic-to-noise ratio as speech biomarker for fatigue: K-nearest neighbour machine learning algorithm.** Gaur S, Kalani P, Mohan M. *Medical Journal, Armed Forces India*, 2023. PMID: [39734876](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39734876/) · DOI: [10.1016/j.mjafi.2022.12.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mjafi.2022.12.001). **N = 32** healthy young Indian male volunteers, within-subject one-night sleep-deprivation design. HNR changed significantly (p<0.05) at 3am relative to baseline. **Small sample, single sex — flag clearly.**

- **Relationship Between Sleep Quality and Stress with Voice Functioning among College Professors: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis.** Carrillo-Gonzalez A, Camargo-Mendoza M, Cantor-Cutiva LC. *Journal of Voice*, 2019. PMID: [31866075](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31866075/) · DOI: [10.1016/j.jvoice.2019.11.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jvoice.2019.11.001). Already logged in Section 3; the one source here that studies sleep quality and stress together against voice outcomes in the same population, rather than joining the two literatures only by shared construct. The authors flag low study quality and low study count themselves — treat as a weak-evidence association, explicitly, same as Section 3 says.

- **Sleep Debt at the Community Level: Impact of Age, Sex, Race/Ethnicity and Health.** Fox EC, Wang K, Aquino M, Grandner MA, Xie D, Branas CC, Gooneratne NS. *Sleep Health*, 2018. PMID: [30031523](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30031523/). **N = 8,752**, phone survey, sleep debt operationalized as self-reported sleep required minus sleep achieved. No voice component — used here only to source the standard definition of sleep debt quoted on the docs page, and to note that sleep debt correlates with self-reported stress at the population level.

- **Perceived daily sleep need and sleep debt in adolescents: associations with daily affect over school and vacation periods.** Shen L, et al. *Sleep*, 2021. PMID: [34323993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34323993/). **N = 205**, actigraphy plus daily diary across school and vacation weeks, cross-lagged multilevel models. Finding: higher cumulative sleep debt predicted higher next-day high-arousal negative affect and lower next-day low-arousal positive affect, even controlling for lagged affect and day of week. No voice component. **This is the best-powered, most directly relevant non-voice source in this section** — it is a real longitudinal design, not a cross-sectional association, and it is the primary citation behind the stress/anxiety/mood/emotional-destabilization entries in the sleep composite.

- **Treatise on an alternative perspective on the two-process model of sleep regulation.** Bes F. *npj Biological Timing and Sleep*, 2025. PMID: [41775810](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41775810/) · DOI: [10.1038/s44323-025-00038-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44323-025-00038-0). Review/perspective piece, not primary data. Used only to source the standard definition of "process S" (the homeostatic sleep-wake drive, informally "sleep debt") that the docs page's opening paragraph references. No voice component, no claim transferred beyond the definition itself.

- **The cumulative cost of additional wakefulness: dose-response effects on neurobehavioral functions and sleep physiology from chronic sleep restriction and total sleep deprivation.** Van Dongen HP, Maislin G, Mullington JM, Dinges DF. *Sleep*, 2003. PMID: [12683469](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12683469/). The landmark chronic-sleep-restriction dose-response study (psychomotor vigilance task performance degrades progressively across days of 4h/6h time-in-bed restriction, without full subjective awareness of the deficit). No voice component — used to back the cognitive-load, attention-dysregulation and cognitive-impairment entries in the sleep composite, where the Serasona sign's own voice evidence is separately documented as thin (see Section 5 above and the Models page's own "limited evidence" note on Clarity).

- **Accuracy of 11 Wearable, Nearable, and Airable Consumer Sleep Trackers: Prospective Multicenter Validation Study.** Lee T, et al. *JMIR mHealth and uHealth*, 2023. PMID: [37917155](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37917155/). **N = 75**, 543 hours of polysomnography compared against 11 consumer sleep trackers. Macro F1 scores against PSG ranged 0.26–0.69 across devices — substantial disagreement even among devices purpose-built to measure sleep. No voice component. Used on the docs page's "What this is not" section as a calibration point: if dedicated sleep-tracking hardware disagrees with PSG this much, a composite that was never designed to measure sleep at all warrants at least as much caution.

**Found but not included, per this document's own verification bar:** Zhang B, et al., "Detecting Sleep Deficiency with Voice Biomarkers and Machine Learning" (conference abstract, *SLEEP* 2024, meeting supplement). Trained an SVM on acoustic features (jitter, shimmer, MFCCs, Cepstral Spectral Index of Dysphonia) to classify chronic sleep restriction vs. baseline in a within-participant controlled trial, reporting AUC 0.76. **N = 5 participants.** No PubMed-indexed PMID could be located for this abstract at the time of review, and five participants is too small a sample to anchor a product claim. Excluded on the same basis Section 7 already excludes the Warthe aircrew study and the Christodoulides thesis — not peer-reviewed-and-indexed, or too small, respectively.
